A Wedding in the Family

Last spring, my older cousin Huong got married. She is the daughter of my mother's older sister, and she is like a big sister to me. We grew up together, played together, and shared many secrets. So when she told me that she was getting married, I was very happy for her. She asked me to be one of her bridesmaids. I said yes immediately, even before she finished asking.

Huong met her husband, Minh, at work. They both worked at the same bank, in different departments. At first, they were just colleagues. They said hello in the elevator and sometimes had lunch together with other people from the office. Then, one day, there was a big storm, and Huong did not have a raincoat. Minh offered to drive her home on his motorbike. They talked the whole way, and they discovered that they had a lot in common. They both loved old movies, spicy food, and long walks. After that day, they started to spend more and more time together. Two years later, Minh asked Huong to marry him. He did it in a small restaurant where they had their first date. He was so nervous that he dropped the ring under the table, and they both had to get down on the floor to find it. Huong laughed so hard that she cried. Then she said yes.

In my country, a wedding is not just about two people. It is about two families. The preparations started many months before the wedding day. First, there was the engagement ceremony. Minh's family came to Huong's house to ask for permission for the two young people to marry. They brought gifts on red trays: fruit, cakes, tea, wine, and betel leaves. The gifts were carried by young unmarried men and women from Minh's family, all wearing traditional clothes. The two families sat together, drank tea, and talked. The oldest man in Minh's family made a speech, asking Huong's parents to accept their son. Huong's father made a speech in return, accepting the gifts and giving his permission. Then the two families had lunch together. It was a very formal and serious event, but also very warm and happy.

After the engagement, the two families chose a date for the wedding. My aunt went to see a fortune teller at a temple to find a lucky day. The fortune teller looked at the birth dates of Huong and Minh and chose a Sunday in March. Then the real work began. There were so many things to do. They had to book a restaurant for the party, choose a menu, send invitations, rent clothes, hire a photographer, buy rings, decorate the house, and much more. Huong and Minh were very busy and very stressed. Sometimes they argued about small things, like the color of the invitations or the number of guests. My aunt said that this is normal. "Every couple argues before the wedding," she said. "It is a good test for the marriage."

The number of guests was a big problem. In my culture, weddings are usually very big. You invite not only your family and close friends, but also your neighbors, your colleagues, your parents' friends, your parents' colleagues, and many other people. Huong and Minh wanted a small wedding with about one hundred guests. But their parents had a different idea. In the end, there were more than four hundred guests. Huong did not know half of them.

A week before the wedding, I went to stay at my aunt's house to help with the preparations. The house was full of people: aunts, uncles, cousins, and neighbors. Everyone had a job. The men put up a big tent in front of the house and hung red lanterns and flowers. The women cooked food, cleaned the house, and prepared the gifts. The children ran around and got in everyone's way. There was noise, laughter, and the smell of good food everywhere. It was tiring, but it was also a lot of fun. I felt like the whole family was coming together for something important.

On the morning of the wedding day, I woke up at four o'clock. The bridesmaids had to be ready very early. A makeup artist came to the house to do our hair and makeup. Huong sat in front of the mirror in her room while the artist worked on her face. She looked calm, but I could see that her hands were shaking. I held one of her hands and told her that everything would be perfect. She smiled at me and said, "I'm so glad you're here."

For the first part of the wedding, Huong wore a traditional red ao dai, a long dress with a matching red headpiece. She looked so beautiful that her mother started to cry as soon as she saw her. My uncle had tears in his eyes too, but he pretended that he had something in his eye. The bridesmaids wore pink ao dai. We felt like princesses.

At eight o'clock, Minh's family arrived. Minh came with his parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins. Again, the young people carried gifts on red trays. Minh wore a dark blue ao dai and looked handsome but very nervous. When he saw Huong, his face lit up, and he smiled the biggest smile I have ever seen. The two families met in the living room, in front of the family altar. Huong and Minh lit incense and bowed to the altar to show respect to Huong's ancestors and ask for their blessing. Then they bowed to Huong's parents and grandparents. My grandmother gave Huong a pair of gold earrings that she had worn on her own wedding day, more than fifty years ago. Huong cried, and my grandmother cried, and soon everyone in the room was crying.

After the ceremony at Huong's house, everyone went to Minh's house, where a similar ceremony took place in front of his family's altar. By the time it was over, it was almost lunchtime, and everyone was hungry. But the biggest event of the day was still to come: the wedding party in the evening.

The party was in a large restaurant in the city center. When we arrived, the room was already full of people. There were about forty round tables, each with ten chairs. The tables were covered with white cloths and decorated with flowers and candles. At the front of the room, there was a stage with a big screen that showed photos of Huong and Minh. For the party, Huong changed into a white Western-style wedding dress, and Minh wore a black suit. They looked like a couple from a movie.

The party started with music and a speech from a host. Then Huong and Minh walked into the room together, holding hands, while everyone clapped and cheered. They walked to the stage and stood in front of their parents. Minh's father gave a speech, thanking everyone for coming and wishing the couple a happy life. Then Huong and Minh cut a tall white wedding cake, poured wine into a tower of glasses, and drank a toast together. Everyone cheered again.

Then the food started to arrive. There were many courses: soup, fried spring rolls, roast chicken, steamed fish, beef, shrimp, fried rice, and fruit for dessert. The waiters brought dish after dish. While the guests ate, Huong and Minh went from table to table to thank everyone for coming. At each table, the guests stood up, raised their glasses, and said "Cheers!" They also gave the couple envelopes with money inside. This is a wedding tradition in my country. The money helps the young couple start their new life together.

There was also music and singing. A band played popular songs, and some of the guests went up on stage to sing karaoke. My uncle sang an old love song for my aunt. His voice was terrible, but it was so sweet that everyone clapped loudly. Some of Huong's friends from university sang a funny song that they had written about Huong and Minh. Everyone laughed. Then the music changed, and people started to dance. Even my grandmother danced a little with my grandfather.

The party ended at about ten o'clock. The guests slowly went home, and the family stayed to clean up. Huong and Minh were exhausted. Huong took off her high shoes and walked barefoot. Her feet hurt, her face hurt from smiling, and her voice was almost gone from talking to so many people. But she was very happy. Before she left with Minh, she hugged me and said, "Thank you for everything. This was the best day of my life."

Two days later, Huong and Minh went on their honeymoon to a small island in the south. They sent us photos of white beaches, blue water, and beautiful sunsets. Now they live in a small apartment near their bank. Last month, they told the family that they are going to have a baby. My aunt was so happy that she started buying baby clothes the next day.

When I think about Huong's wedding, I remember the beautiful clothes, the delicious food, and the music. But most of all, I remember the people: the two families coming together, the tears and the laughter, my grandmother's gold earrings, and the look on Minh's face when he first saw Huong in her red ao dai. A wedding is not just a party. It is the beginning of a new family, and the joining of two old ones.
