Tet, the Lunar New Year

Tet is the most important holiday of the year in my country. It is the Lunar New Year, and it usually happens in late January or February. For us, Tet is like Christmas, New Year, and everyone's birthday all at the same time. It is a time for family, for remembering our ancestors, for eating special food, and for hoping for good luck in the new year. Every year, millions of people travel back to their hometowns to celebrate Tet with their families. The roads, the buses, the trains, and the airports are full. The big cities become strangely quiet and empty, because so many people have gone home.

Preparations for Tet start weeks before the holiday. The first thing everyone does is clean the house. People believe that cleaning the house before Tet sweeps away the bad luck of the old year and makes room for good luck in the new year. In my family, we clean everything: the floors, the windows, the walls, the furniture, the kitchen, and the family altar. We also throw away old things that we do not need anymore. My mother is very serious about this. She says that you should never start a new year in a dirty house. Some families even paint their houses before Tet, so they look fresh and new.

After cleaning, we decorate the house. The most important decorations are flowers and trees. In the north of the country, people usually buy a peach tree with pink flowers. In the south, people prefer a yellow apricot tree. We also buy a kumquat tree, which is a small tree with many little orange fruits. People believe that the fruits bring wealth and good luck. A few days before Tet, the streets are full of flower markets. There are flowers of every color everywhere. It is one of the most beautiful times of the year. You can see people carrying big peach trees and kumquat trees on their motorbikes through the busy streets. It always looks dangerous, but somehow they never drop them.

We also hang red decorations around the house. Red is the color of luck and happiness in my culture. We hang red paper with good wishes written in gold letters on the walls and doors. Some wishes are for health, some for wealth, and some for peace and happiness. My father also buys a picture for the new year, usually of the animal for that year. Every year has an animal from the zodiac: the rat, the buffalo, the tiger, the cat, the dragon, the snake, the horse, the goat, the monkey, the rooster, the dog, and the pig. I was born in the year of the dragon, and my brother was born in the year of the horse.

One of the most important parts of preparing for Tet is cooking. Tet food is special, and many dishes are only made at this time of year. The most famous is a square cake made of sticky rice, green beans, and pork, wrapped in green leaves. In the south, people make a similar cake, but it is long and round instead of square. Making these cakes is a family activity. In my family, we always make them together, two days before Tet. My grandmother is the expert, and she teaches all of us. We wash the leaves, soak the rice and beans, and cut the pork. Then we carefully put everything together and wrap the cakes with the leaves and tie them with string. My grandmother's cakes are always perfect squares. Mine are always a little crooked, and everyone laughs at them. Then we put the cakes in a giant pot of water and boil them for about ten to twelve hours. Someone has to watch the fire all night and add more water when it gets low. When I was a child, I loved staying up late with my father to watch the fire. We sat outside in the cold night air, talking, eating sweets, and listening to the water bubble in the pot. These are some of my happiest memories.

We also make other special Tet dishes: pickled onions and vegetables, pork cooked slowly with eggs in coconut water, boiled chicken, spring rolls, and a soup made with bamboo shoots. There are also many kinds of sweets: candied ginger, candied coconut, dried fruits, and roasted seeds. We put the sweets on a special tray with many small sections and offer them to guests who visit during Tet.

The last day of the old year is very busy and very important. In the afternoon, we prepare a big meal and put it on the family altar as an offering to our ancestors. We light incense and invite their spirits to come home and celebrate Tet with us. My father, as the oldest son, stands in front of the altar and prays. He tells our ancestors about the year that has passed and asks them to protect our family in the new year. Then everyone in the family takes turns to bow in front of the altar. After that, we sit down together and eat the meal. It is a quiet, serious, and warm moment. I always feel close to my grandparents who have passed away during this time.

At midnight on New Year's Eve, there is a special moment called Giao Thua, which means the moment when the old year ends and the new year begins. In many cities, there are fireworks. My family usually watches them on television, or sometimes we go out to a park to see them in person. The sky lights up with colors, and everyone cheers. Then we go home and pray at the altar again to welcome the new year.

The first person who enters your house in the new year is very important. People believe that this person will decide your family's luck for the whole year. So families often choose someone who is kind, successful, and has a good character to be the first visitor. In my family, my uncle always comes to our house first, very early on the first morning of Tet. He is a cheerful man with a big laugh and a good job, so my parents think he brings good luck. He always arrives with a big smile and a bag of oranges.

The first three days of Tet are for visiting. On the first day, we visit my father's family. On the second day, we visit my mother's family. On the third day, we visit teachers, friends, and neighbors. Everybody wears new clothes, because new clothes are a symbol of a fresh start. When we visit someone's house, we wish them a happy new year and good things for the coming year: good health, success in work, wealth, and happiness. They offer us tea, sweets, and food. We sit and talk for a while, and then we go to the next house. By the end of the day, we have eaten so much food that we can hardly move.

For children, the best part of Tet is lucky money. Adults give children small red envelopes with money inside. When I was a child, I could not wait for Tet because of the lucky money. My brother and I always compared how much we got. Before an adult gives you the envelope, you have to wish them a happy new year. Children usually say something like, "I wish you good health and a long life." Now that I have a job, I am the one who gives lucky money to the younger children in my family. It feels strange, but also very nice.

There are also many beliefs and customs about what you should and should not do during Tet. You should not sweep the floor during the first three days, because you might sweep away your good luck. You should not argue or say bad words, because it will bring bad luck for the whole year. You should not break anything, especially glass or plates. You should not lend or borrow money. And you should try to be happy and smile a lot, because how you feel at the beginning of the year is how you will feel for the rest of the year. Some young people do not believe in these customs anymore, but most families still follow them, at least a little.

Many people also go to temples and pagodas during Tet. They go to pray for good luck, health, and happiness for their families. The temples are very crowded, full of people and the smell of incense. My grandmother always takes the whole family to a pagoda on the first or second day of Tet. We light incense, pray, and sometimes ask for a fortune. Then we buy a small branch of a tree or a few flowers to take home for good luck.

Tet usually lasts about a week, but for many people, the feeling of Tet continues for a whole month. After the holiday, people slowly go back to the cities and back to work. The streets become busy again, and life returns to normal. But for a few days every year, everything stops. People leave their work and their worries behind, and they go home to be with the people they love. They remember the past, celebrate the present, and hope for the future.

I have lived in the city for many years now, and I am very busy with my job. Sometimes I forget to call my parents for a week or two. But every year, when Tet comes, I take the long bus ride back to my hometown. When I walk into my parents' house and smell the incense, the flowers, and my mother's cooking, I know that I am home.
