Taking the Bus Across the City

For many years, I went everywhere on my motorbike. It was fast and easy, and I could go wherever I wanted, whenever I wanted. But last year, my motorbike was stolen from outside a coffee shop. I was very upset. A new motorbike was too expensive for me at that time, so I decided to try the bus for a few months. I thought it would be terrible. I was wrong. Today, I still take the bus every day, even though I have enough money to buy a new motorbike. Let me tell you why.

My first day on the bus was not easy. I did not know which bus to take or where the bus stops were. I asked my neighbor, an old man who takes the bus every day, and he helped me. He showed me an app on my phone that tells you which bus to take, where to wait, and when the next bus will arrive. "It is very simple," he said. "You type where you are and where you want to go, and the app does the rest."

The next morning, I left my house at seven o'clock and walked to the bus stop at the end of my street. It took about five minutes. There were already six or seven people waiting: two students in school uniforms, a woman with a big bag of vegetables, a young man in a suit, and an old woman with a small dog in a basket. Nobody talked. Most people were looking at their phones. I checked the app. My bus, number 32, would arrive in four minutes.

When the bus came, everybody got on through the front door. I was not sure what to do, so I watched the person in front of me. She showed a card to the driver and walked to the back. I did not have a card, so I asked the driver how much the ticket was. "Seven thousand," he said. I gave him the money, and he gave me a small paper ticket. Later, I learned that you can buy a monthly pass that is much cheaper if you take the bus every day.

The bus was quite full, and there were no empty seats. I had to stand and hold onto a bar above my head. At first, I was a little worried. The bus moved slowly through the heavy traffic, and every time it stopped, I almost lost my balance. But after a few stops, I got used to it. I looked around. There were people of all ages: office workers, students, grandmothers, a few tourists with big backpacks. A young woman was reading a book. An old man was sleeping with his head against the window. Two school girls were sharing a pair of headphones and laughing quietly.

My office is on the other side of the city, about twelve kilometers from my home. On my motorbike, the trip took about thirty minutes. On the bus, it took almost an hour, and I had to change buses once. At first, this seemed like a big waste of time. But then I realized something important. On my motorbike, I had to pay attention to the road every second. I had to watch the cars, the other motorbikes, the people crossing the street, the traffic lights. When I got to work, I was always tired and stressed. On the bus, someone else was driving. I could relax.

So I started to use my time on the bus in different ways. On some days, I read a book. In the past year, I have read more books than in the five years before. On other days, I listen to podcasts or learn English with an app on my phone. Sometimes I just look out of the window and watch the city. I have noticed so many things that I never saw before from my motorbike: a beautiful old house with a red door, a small park where old people do exercises every morning, a tiny shop that sells the best bread in the neighborhood, a street full of flower sellers.

I have also met some interesting people on the bus. There is an old woman who takes the same bus as me every morning. She goes to a temple in the city center to pray. At first, we only smiled at each other. Then one day, she offered me a piece of candy, and we started to talk. Now, we chat every morning. She tells me about her grandchildren, and I tell her about my work. Once, when I was sick and did not take the bus for a few days, she asked the driver about me. When I came back, she gave me a small bag of oranges. "For your health," she said.

There is also a young man who plays the guitar at the bus station where I change buses. He is a university student, and he plays music to earn a little extra money. He is very good. Every morning, I put a few coins in his guitar case. Sometimes, if I am early, I stay and listen to a song or two. It is a nice way to start the day.

Of course, the bus is not always pleasant. During rush hour, it can be very crowded, especially in the evening. Sometimes there are so many people that you cannot move at all. In the hot season, even with the air conditioner on, the bus can be uncomfortable. And sometimes, the bus is late. Once, I waited for forty minutes in the rain because there was an accident on the road. I was very late for work, and my clothes were wet. On days like that, I miss my motorbike.

There are also some rules on the bus that not everyone follows. You should give your seat to old people, pregnant women, people with small children, and people with disabilities. Most people do this without being asked. Once, I saw a teenage boy jump up from his seat when an old man got on the bus. The old man thanked him with a big smile. But sometimes, people pretend they are sleeping or busy with their phones so they don't have to stand up. You also should not talk too loudly, eat food with a strong smell, or play music without headphones. Most people are polite, but not everyone.

One day, something happened on the bus that I will never forget. It was a Friday evening, and the bus was very crowded. A small boy, maybe five or six years old, was standing near the door. He was crying. He had got on the bus with his mother, but she did not get on in time, and the doors closed. The boy was alone and very scared. Immediately, several passengers came to help. A woman held his hand and talked to him softly. A man went to tell the driver. The driver stopped the bus at the next stop and called the bus company. In less than fifteen minutes, the boy's mother arrived on a motorbike taxi. She was crying too. She hugged her son tightly and thanked everyone on the bus again and again. When the bus started to move again, all the passengers were smiling. For a moment, we were not strangers. We were a small community.

Taking the bus has also helped me save money. I don't have to pay for gas, parking, or repairs. My monthly bus pass costs less than one tank of gas for a motorbike. In one year, I have saved enough money to take a short trip to the beach with my family. I also feel good that I am helping the environment a little. One bus can carry fifty or sixty people. If all those people drove their own motorbikes, the air in our city would be even dirtier, and the traffic would be even worse.

My friends often ask me why I don't buy a new motorbike. "The bus is so slow," they say. "And it's so crowded." They are right. The bus is slower, and it is often crowded. But I have learned that faster is not always better. On the bus, I have time to read, to think, and to rest. I see my city with new eyes. I have made friends with people I would never have met. And I arrive at work calm instead of stressed.

My neighbor, the old man who helped me on my first day, once said something that I still remember. "When you drive, you only see the road," he told me. "When you take the bus, you see the people." I did not understand what he meant at that time. Now I do. Tomorrow morning, I will walk to the bus stop at the end of my street, wait for bus number 32, and say good morning to the old woman who goes to the temple. And I will smile, because I know it will be a good day.
