Cleaning the House on Sunday

In our family, Sunday morning is cleaning time. It has been like this since I was a little girl. During the week, everyone is busy with work and school, so the house slowly becomes messy. There are shoes by the door, books on the table, clothes on the chairs, and dust on every shelf. By Saturday night, our home does not look very nice. So every Sunday, after breakfast, my mother stands in the middle of the living room, claps her hands, and says, "Okay, everyone! It's time to clean!"

When I was a child, I hated Sunday cleaning. I wanted to watch cartoons on television or play with my friends outside. I always tried to find an excuse. "I have a lot of homework," I would say. Or, "My stomach hurts." But my mother always knew when I was lying. She would just smile and give me a cloth and a bucket of water. Now that I am older, I understand why she did it. Cleaning together taught us to work as a team and to take care of our home. And, to be honest, I now enjoy it a little.

My mother is the leader of the cleaning team. At the beginning, she writes a list of jobs and gives each person a task. She says that if everybody knows exactly what to do, the work will be faster and nobody will argue. Last Sunday, the list looked like this: Dad cleans the bathroom and the kitchen. Mai cleans the bedrooms and does the laundry. Nam takes out the trash, cleans the windows, and waters the plants. Grandma sorts the old newspapers and folds the clean clothes. Mom sweeps and mops all the floors.

My father always gets the bathroom, and he always complains about it. "Why is it always me?" he says. My mother answers, "Because you are the best at it." My father shakes his head, but he goes to the bathroom anyway. He puts on a pair of rubber gloves, takes a brush and a bottle of cleaner, and starts to scrub the toilet, the sink, and the shower. He sings old songs while he works. He is not a very good singer, but his singing makes the work more fun for everybody.

I start with my own bedroom, because it is the messiest room in the house. I am not a very tidy person, I must say. During the week, I put my clothes on my chair instead of in the closet. I leave cups and plates on my desk. There are books and papers everywhere. On Sunday, I have to face the mess. First, I open the window to let fresh air in. Then I pick up all the clothes. Some are clean, so I fold them and put them in the closet. Some are dirty, so I put them in the laundry basket. Next, I take all the cups and plates to the kitchen. Then I organize my desk. I put the books back on the shelf and throw away old papers that I don't need anymore.

After that, I change the sheets on my bed. My mother says we must wash our sheets and pillow covers every week, especially in the hot season, when we sweat a lot at night. I take off the old sheets and put on clean ones. I love the smell of clean sheets. On Sunday night, my bed always feels softer and more comfortable than on any other night.

Then I dust the furniture. I use a soft cloth to clean the top of my desk, my bookshelf, the window, and the lamp. I am always surprised by how much dust there is after only one week. Our house is near a busy road, and dust from the street comes in through the windows every day. When I finish my room, I go to my brother's room. His room is even messier than mine. There are toys, comic books, and socks all over the floor. I find a half-eaten cookie under his bed. "Nam!" I shout. "Come and look at this!" He runs in, sees the cookie, and laughs. "Oh, I was looking for that!" he says. I make him pick up all his toys and socks before I continue.

While I am cleaning the bedrooms, the washing machine is working. I put in the first load of laundry: white shirts, sheets, and towels. Our washing machine is old and very loud. When it spins, the whole bathroom shakes. My father always says that we need a new one, but my mother says that it still works fine, so there is no reason to waste money. When the machine stops, I take the wet clothes out and hang them on the clothesline on our balcony. The sun is strong, so the clothes will be dry in two or three hours. Then I put in the second load: dark clothes, jeans, and socks.

My brother's first job is to take out the trash. In our neighborhood, we must separate our rubbish into different bags. One bag is for food waste, one bag is for plastic and paper that can be recycled, and one bag is for everything else. At first, my brother did not care about this. He put everything in one bag. But last year, his teacher at school talked to the class about the environment and about how much plastic ends up in the ocean. Now, my brother is very serious about recycling. He checks every bag very carefully. Sometimes he even tells my father off. "Dad! This plastic bottle doesn't go in that bag!" My father laughs and says, "Yes, sir!"

Next, my brother cleans the windows. He uses a spray bottle with water and a little soap, and he wipes the glass with old newspapers. My grandmother taught him this trick. She says newspaper is better than a cloth because it doesn't leave any marks on the glass. My brother likes this job because he can stand on a chair and look outside. Sometimes he stops working to watch the people and motorbikes in the street. My mother has to remind him to keep going.

His last job is to water the plants. We have many plants on our balcony and in front of the house. There are flowers, herbs, a small lemon tree, and some chili plants. My grandmother loves her plants, and she talks to them every morning. She believes that plants grow better when you talk to them kindly. My brother thinks this is funny, but he still says "good morning" to the lemon tree when he waters it.

My grandmother's job is easy, because she is old and we don't want her to work too hard. She sits in the living room and sorts the old newspapers and magazines into piles. Some of them she keeps, because there are recipes or interesting stories she wants to read again. The rest we will sell to the man who comes around the neighborhood on his bike every week to buy old paper and bottles. Later, when the clothes are dry, my grandmother folds them. She folds them very neatly, much better than any of us can. Every shirt, every towel, and every pair of pants becomes a perfect square.

My mother does the floors last, because she wants all the dust from the other jobs to fall on the floor first. She starts by sweeping every room with a big broom. Then she fills a bucket with warm water and a little floor cleaner and mops the whole house, starting from the bedrooms and finishing at the front door. While the floors are wet, nobody can walk inside. So we all sit on the balcony together and wait for the floors to dry.

This is my favorite part of Sunday cleaning. We are all a little tired and sweaty, but the hard work is finished. My mother brings out a big plate of cut fruit: watermelon, mango, and pineapple. My father makes a pot of tea. My grandmother tells us about how people cleaned their homes when she was young. "We had no washing machine," she says. "We washed every piece of clothing by hand, in the river. It took the whole day!" My brother cannot believe it. "The whole day? Just for washing?" he asks. My grandmother nods. "You children are very lucky," she says.

After about half an hour, the floors are dry, and we go back inside. The house looks completely different. Everything is clean, bright, and in its right place. The floors shine, the windows are clear, and the air smells fresh. My mother looks around with a happy face. "Good job, team," she says. "Now, who wants to help me cook lunch?"

Sometimes my friends ask me why we don't hire someone to clean our house. Some families in our city do that. But my mother says that cleaning your own home is important. It teaches you responsibility. It helps you appreciate the things you have. And when you clean together, you also spend time together. I think she is right. Some of my happiest memories are from these Sunday mornings: my father singing in the bathroom, my brother talking to the lemon tree, my grandmother's perfect towels, and all of us sitting together on the balcony, eating fruit and waiting for the floors to dry.
