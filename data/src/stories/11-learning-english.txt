Learning English

I started learning English when I was eight years old. Like many children in my country, I studied it at school two or three times a week. Our teacher was a kind woman with a soft voice, but she could not speak English very well herself. We spent most of our time reading words from a book and repeating them after her. "Apple. Banana. Cat. Dog." We learned many words and many grammar rules, but we almost never spoke English with real people. After ten years of English classes, I could read a little and write a little, but I could not have a simple conversation. When a foreign tourist asked me for directions in the street once, I was so nervous that I could not say anything. I just pointed and smiled. I felt very embarrassed.

That day, I decided that I really wanted to learn English. Not just for tests and exams, but for real life. I wanted to talk to people from other countries, watch movies without subtitles, read books in English, and maybe one day travel or work abroad. But I did not know how to start. I did not have much money for expensive classes. So I started to look for other ways to learn.

The first thing I did was ask for advice. I talked to my cousin Minh, who works for an international company and speaks English very well. I asked him how he learned. He laughed and said, "There is no magic secret. You just have to use English every day, a little bit at a time. Read, listen, speak, and write. And don't be afraid of making mistakes." He also gave me some practical advice. "Start with simple things that you enjoy," he said. "If you like music, listen to English songs. If you like movies, watch English movies. Learning is much easier when it is fun."

So I made a plan. Every day, I would spend at least one hour with English. It did not have to be at the same time or in the same way. It could be thirty minutes in the morning and thirty minutes at night. The most important thing was to do it every day, even when I was tired or busy.

I started with listening. Every morning, while I was getting ready for school, I listened to English songs. At first, I did not understand most of the words. But I liked the music, so I listened again and again. Then I looked up the lyrics on the internet and read them while I listened. Slowly, I started to understand more and more. I learned new words and phrases from the songs, and I started to sing along. My little brother thought I was crazy, singing in English in the bathroom every morning. But I did not care.

Then I started watching movies and TV shows in English. At first, I used subtitles in my own language. Then, after a few months, I changed to English subtitles. This was much harder, but it helped me a lot. I could hear the words and see them at the same time. When I heard a new word or an interesting phrase, I wrote it down in a small notebook. Later, I looked it up in the dictionary and wrote the meaning next to it. By the end of the first year, my notebook had more than a thousand new words and phrases.

Reading was more difficult for me. At first, I tried to read a famous English novel, but it was much too hard. There were new words in every sentence, and I had to use the dictionary all the time. After ten pages, I gave up. Then my cousin told me about graded readers. These are books written in simple English for learners. They have different levels, from very easy to more difficult. I started with the easiest level. The stories were simple, but they were interesting, and I could understand almost everything. When I finished one book, I started another. Slowly, I moved up to higher levels. After about two years, I could read a real English novel. It was still a little difficult, but I could enjoy it.

I also learned something very important about reading. You do not need to understand every word. If you understand most of the words in a sentence, you can usually guess the meaning of the new ones. Stopping to look up every word makes reading slow and boring. So I made a rule for myself. I only looked up a word if I saw it three times, or if I could not understand the sentence without it. This made reading much more pleasant.

Speaking was the hardest part. I did not have any English-speaking friends, and I was too shy to talk to tourists. So, at first, I practiced alone. I talked to myself in English while I was cooking, cleaning, or walking to school. I described what I was doing. "Now I am washing the dishes. The water is hot. This plate is very dirty." It sounds silly, but it really helped me. It made me think in English instead of translating from my own language.

Then I found an English club at a coffee shop near my university. Every Saturday afternoon, people met there to practice speaking English. Some were students like me. Some were office workers. There were also a few foreigners who lived in the city and wanted to meet local people. The first time I went, I was terribly nervous. My hands were shaking, and my heart was beating fast. I sat in a corner and did not say anything for the first hour. Then a friendly young woman from Canada sat next to me and asked me about my studies. I answered slowly, making many mistakes. But she was very patient. She smiled, listened, and helped me when I could not find the right word. At the end of the afternoon, she said, "Your English is good! You should come again next week." I felt like I was flying.

I went to the English club every Saturday for two years. I made many friends there, both Vietnamese and foreign. We talked about everything: food, travel, music, movies, our families, our dreams. My speaking improved a lot. I became more confident. I made fewer mistakes, and when I did make a mistake, I was not embarrassed anymore. I understood that mistakes are a normal part of learning. Even native speakers make mistakes sometimes.

Writing was the last skill I worked on. I started to keep a diary in English. Every night before bed, I wrote a few sentences about my day. At first, my diary entries were very short and simple. "Today I went to school. I had rice and fish for lunch. I was tired." But over time, they became longer and more interesting. I wrote about my feelings, my problems, and my hopes. Sometimes I showed my diary to my friend from Canada, and she helped me correct my mistakes. Writing helped me remember new words and grammar rules, because I had to use them myself.

There were times when I wanted to give up. Sometimes I felt like I was not making any progress. I studied every day, but I still did not understand fast English, and I still made the same mistakes again and again. On those days, I felt stupid and hopeless. But then I remembered how far I had come. I looked at my first diary entries and compared them with my latest ones. I remembered the day when I could not answer the tourist's simple question. And I realized that I had made a lot of progress. It was just slow, so I could not see it every day.

Today, five years after that embarrassing day with the tourist, I speak English quite well. It is not perfect, and I still have an accent, but I can have long conversations, read books and news, watch movies, and write emails without much difficulty. Last year, I got a job at a travel company, where I use English every day with customers from all over the world. My boss told me that my English was one of the main reasons she hired me.

If you are learning English, here is my advice. First, use English every day, even if it is only for fifteen minutes. A little bit every day is better than a lot once a week. Second, choose things that you enjoy: songs, movies, books, games, anything. Third, don't be afraid of mistakes. Everyone makes them, and they are the best way to learn. Fourth, find people to practice with, even if it is just yourself in the mirror. And finally, be patient. Learning a language is like growing a tree. You plant the seed, you water it every day, and for a long time, you see nothing. But one day, you look up, and there is a big, beautiful tree in front of you.
