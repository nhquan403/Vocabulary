Visiting Grandparents in the Countryside

My father's parents live in a small village in the countryside, about three hours from the city by bus. When I was a child, I spent every summer holiday with them. Now that I am an adult and have a job, I cannot stay for a whole summer anymore. But I still try to visit them for a few days every two or three months. Last month, I took a week off work and went to stay with them. It reminded me how different life in the countryside is from life in the city, and how much I love it.

I took an early bus from the city on a Saturday morning. The bus was old and noisy, and the seats were not very comfortable. But I enjoyed the trip. As we left the city, the tall buildings and crowded streets slowly disappeared. Then there were small towns, then fields, then villages surrounded by bamboo trees. The air coming through the window became cleaner and cooler. I could smell the earth and the rice fields. I put away my phone and just looked outside.

The bus stopped at the side of a main road, near a small shop. From there, I had to walk about two kilometers along a narrow dirt road to get to my grandparents' village. When I got off the bus, I saw my grandfather waiting for me under a tree. He is eighty years old, but he is still strong. He had come on his old bicycle to carry my bag. "You are thinner than last time," he said, looking at me. "Your grandmother will be worried. She will make you eat a lot this week." I laughed. I knew he was right.

We walked along the road together, my grandfather pushing his bicycle with my bag on the back. On both sides of the road, there were rice fields as far as I could see. The rice was young and bright green, and it moved like waves in the wind. Some farmers were working in the fields, bent over, with their feet in the water. They wore cone hats to protect themselves from the sun. When they saw my grandfather, they called out and waved. Everybody in the village knows him. He was the village teacher for forty years, and he taught almost every adult in the village to read and write.

My grandparents' house is at the end of the village, near a small river. It is a traditional house, made of wood and brick, with a red tile roof. It is more than one hundred years old. My grandfather's grandfather built it. In front of the house, there is a big yard where my grandmother dries rice and vegetables in the sun. There is also a garden with fruit trees: bananas, mangoes, a jackfruit tree, and a big old longan tree that gives sweet fruit in the summer. Behind the house, there is a pond with fish, and a small farm area where my grandparents keep chickens, ducks, and a pig.

When we arrived, my grandmother was in the kitchen. She came out, drying her hands on her shirt, and hugged me tightly. She is very small, much shorter than me, but her hug was strong. "Look at you!" she said. "So thin! Don't they have food in the city?" Then she took my hand and led me into the house, where she had already prepared lunch. There was rice, boiled chicken, fish from the pond cooked with sugar and fish sauce, a soup with vegetables from the garden, and some fresh fruit. Everything came from their own farm. Nothing was bought from a supermarket. It was simple food, but it was the most delicious meal I had eaten in months.

Life in the countryside starts very early. On my first morning, I woke up at five o'clock to the sound of the rooster crowing outside my window. At first, I was annoyed. In the city, I usually sleep until seven. But when I went outside, I saw that my grandparents were already up and working. My grandmother was feeding the chickens and ducks. My grandfather was working in the garden. The sky was pink, the air was cool and fresh, and there was a light mist over the rice fields. It was so beautiful and peaceful that I forgot about being tired.

During my week in the village, I tried to help my grandparents as much as I could. In the morning, I helped my grandmother feed the animals and collect eggs. The chickens were not happy when I took their eggs, and one of them tried to bite my hand. My grandmother laughed at me. "You must be gentle but quick," she said. Then I helped my grandfather in the garden. We pulled weeds, watered the vegetables, and picked ripe fruit from the trees. My grandfather knows everything about plants. He told me which plants need a lot of sun and which ones prefer shade, which ones need a lot of water and which ones need only a little. He grows everything without chemicals. "The best way to grow food is the natural way," he said. "If you take care of the soil, the soil will take care of you."

One day, I went to the rice fields with my uncle, who lives in the house next door. It was harvest time for some of the fields. I tried to cut the rice with a small knife, the way my uncle did. It looked easy, but it was very hard work. After only one hour in the hot sun, my back hurt, my hands had blisters, and I was covered in mud. My uncle and the other farmers worked all day like that, from early morning until evening. I had a new respect for them. Every time I eat a bowl of rice now, I think about how much work it took to grow it.

In the afternoons, when it was too hot to work outside, the whole village slowed down. People rested in their houses or in the shade of the trees. My grandmother took a nap in her hammock. My grandfather read his newspaper and drank tea. Sometimes I sat with him and we played Chinese chess. He has played chess for more than sixty years, and he is very good. I lost every game. But he always gave me advice after each game. "You are too fast," he said. "You need to think about your next three moves, not just your next move. It is the same in life."

In the evenings, the village came alive again. The children came out to play in the road. They played football, jumped rope, and flew kites in the fields. Neighbors visited each other's houses to talk and drink tea. Every evening, a few of my grandparents' friends came to sit in the yard. They talked about the weather, the rice, their children and grandchildren, and the news from the village. Who was getting married, who had a new baby, who was sick. In the village, everybody knows everybody, and everybody helps everybody. When someone is sick, the neighbors bring food. When someone builds a house, the whole village helps. When someone dies, the whole village comes to the funeral.

One evening, my grandfather and I went fishing in the river behind the house. We sat on the bank with two long bamboo fishing poles and waited. For a long time, nothing happened. The sun went down, and the sky turned orange. Frogs started to sing. Small birds flew low over the water. My grandfather told me stories about the village when he was young. He told me about the war, about the hard years after the war when there was not enough food, and about how he met my grandmother at a village festival. "She was the most beautiful girl in the village," he said, smiling. "All the young men wanted to marry her. But she chose me, because I could read poems." Just then, my fishing pole moved. I pulled it up, and there was a small silver fish on the end of the line. My grandfather laughed and clapped. We took it home, and my grandmother cooked it for dinner.

At night, the countryside is very dark and very quiet. There are no street lights, no car horns, no loud music. You can see thousands of stars in the sky. On my last night, I lay in the yard on a bamboo bed and looked up at the stars for a long time. In the city, I can only see a few stars, because of the lights and the pollution. Here, the sky was full of them. I could hear frogs, insects, and the wind in the bamboo trees. My grandmother came out and sat next to me. She did not say anything. She just held my hand. It was one of the most peaceful moments of my life.

When it was time to leave, my grandmother filled my bag with food: rice, eggs, vegetables, fruit, and a bottle of homemade fish sauce. My bag was so heavy that I could hardly carry it. My grandfather took me back to the bus stop on his bicycle. Before I got on the bus, he put his hand on my shoulder and said, "Come back soon. We are old now. We don't know how many more visits we will have." I promised him I would.

On the bus back to the city, I thought about my week in the village. Life in the countryside is not easy. The work is hard, the money is little, and there are not many modern things. Many young people from the village have moved to the city to find better jobs. But there is something in the countryside that we have lost in the city: a slower pace of life, a close connection with nature, and a strong sense of community. When I got back to my small apartment in the busy city, I already missed the sound of the rooster in the morning.
