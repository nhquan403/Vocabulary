A Visit to the Doctor

I am not a person who gets sick very often. I eat well, I exercise three times a week, and I usually sleep seven or eight hours every night. In the last five years, I have only had a few colds. So when I started to feel sick last month, I did not pay much attention. I thought it was nothing serious. That was a mistake.

It started on a Tuesday. I woke up with a sore throat and a slight headache. I thought maybe I had slept with the fan on too high, or maybe I had talked too much the day before. I drank a cup of hot tea with honey and lemon, took a pill for my headache, and went to work as usual. During the day, I felt tired, but I thought it was because I had not slept well.

On Wednesday, I felt worse. My throat hurt more, and I started to cough. My whole body ached, especially my back and my legs. In the afternoon, I felt very cold, even though the office was warm. My colleague, Hanh, looked at me and said, "Are you okay? Your face is very red." She touched my forehead with the back of her hand. "You're hot! I think you have a fever. You should go home."

I did not want to go home. We had a lot of work that week, and I did not want to leave my team. But Hanh insisted. She also told our manager, and he agreed with her. "Go home and rest," he said. "If you are not better tomorrow, go to the doctor. Your health is more important than work." So I took a taxi home, went straight to bed, and slept for three hours.

When I woke up, I felt even worse. I took my temperature with the thermometer that my mother had given me when I moved into my own apartment. It was thirty-nine degrees. I had a high fever. I was alone in my apartment, and I felt weak and a little scared. I called my mother. She lives in another city, about two hours away by bus.

"You need to see a doctor," she said immediately. "Don't wait. Go tomorrow morning. And drink a lot of water tonight. Eat some rice soup if you can."

That night was very long. I could not sleep well. I was hot, then cold, then hot again. I coughed a lot, and my throat felt like it was on fire. I drank glass after glass of water. At about midnight, my neighbor, an old woman who lives across the hall, heard me coughing. She knocked on my door and gave me a bowl of hot rice soup with ginger and green onions. "Eat this," she said. "It will help you feel better." I was very grateful. I did not know her very well before that night, but she was so kind to me.

The next morning, I went to a clinic near my home. It was a small private clinic, and it opened at seven o'clock. I arrived at a quarter past seven, but there were already about ten people in the waiting room. There was an old man with a bad cough, a young mother with a crying baby, a boy with a broken arm, and several other people who looked tired and sick. I went to the front desk and gave my name to the receptionist.

"Is this your first visit?" she asked.

"Yes, it is," I answered.

She gave me a form to fill in. I had to write my name, my date of birth, my address, my phone number, and information about my health. There were questions like "Do you have any allergies?" and "Are you taking any medicine at the moment?" I answered all the questions and gave the form back to her. "Please take a seat," she said. "The doctor will call your name."

I sat in the waiting room for almost an hour. There was a television on the wall, but I did not watch it. I was too tired. I just closed my eyes and waited. Sometimes I coughed, and the old man next to me looked at me with sympathy. "This weather," he said, shaking his head. "Hot one day, rainy the next. Everybody is getting sick."

Finally, a nurse called my name. She took me to a small room where she checked my weight, my height, and my blood pressure. She also took my temperature again. It was still thirty-eight point five degrees. She wrote everything down and told me to wait for the doctor.

A few minutes later, the doctor came in. She was a woman of about fifty, with short black hair and a calm, gentle voice. "Good morning," she said with a smile. "What seems to be the problem?"

I told her about my symptoms: the sore throat, the cough, the headache, the fever, and the pain in my body. She listened carefully and asked me some questions. "When did it start? Do you have a runny nose? Are you coughing anything up? Have you been near anyone who was sick?" I answered every question as well as I could.

Then she examined me. She looked inside my mouth and my ears with a small light. She felt the sides of my neck. She listened to my chest and my back with her stethoscope. "Take a deep breath," she said. "Again. Now cough, please." She listened for a long time. Then she put her stethoscope away and sat down at her desk.

"You have a bad throat infection," she said. "Your lungs sound okay, which is good. But the infection is quite serious, and that is why you have a high fever. I will give you some medicine. You need to take it for seven days. It is very important to finish all of it, even if you feel better after three or four days."

She wrote a prescription and explained everything to me. "Take these pills three times a day, after meals. These ones are for your fever and your pain. Take them only when you need them, but not more than four times a day. And this is a syrup for your cough. Take one spoon before bed."

"Can I go to work?" I asked.

She shook her head. "No. You need to rest for at least three days. Stay at home, sleep as much as you can, and drink a lot of water. Warm drinks are good for your throat. Eat soft food, like rice soup or noodle soup. Don't drink anything cold, and don't eat spicy food. If your fever is not better in three days, or if you have trouble breathing, come back immediately."

I thanked the doctor and went to the front desk to pay. The visit was not cheap, but I had health insurance from my company, so I did not have to pay for everything. Then I went to the pharmacy next door. The pharmacist was a young man who explained the medicine to me again, very clearly. He also gave me some sweets for my throat. "These will help with the pain," he said.

When I got home, I sent a message to my manager and told him what the doctor said. He answered quickly. "Don't worry about work. Take care of yourself. We will see you next week." I also called my mother, and she was very relieved. "I told you to see a doctor," she said. "You are always too stubborn."

For the next three days, I stayed in bed. I slept a lot, took my medicine on time, and drank so much water that I lost count of the glasses. My neighbor came to visit me every day. She brought me rice soup, fruit, and once even a small pot of chicken soup. We talked a lot, and I learned that she had been a teacher for thirty years. She had two sons who lived in other countries, and she often felt lonely. I promised to visit her more often when I was better.

On the fourth day, my fever was gone. On the fifth day, my throat did not hurt anymore. On the seventh day, I finished my medicine, and I felt almost normal again. I went back to work on the following Monday. My colleagues were happy to see me. Hanh gave me a small bag of oranges. "For your vitamins," she said.

This experience taught me a few important lessons. First, when your body tells you something is wrong, you should listen. If I had gone to the doctor on the first day, maybe I would not have been so sick. Second, rest is not lazy. Sometimes, the best thing you can do for yourself and for others is to stay at home and get better. And third, kindness can come from unexpected places. Before I was sick, my neighbor was just an old woman I said hello to in the hall. Now, she is my friend. Every Sunday, I visit her, and we drink tea together. She tells me stories about her students, and I tell her about my work. When I think about that terrible week, I don't remember the fever or the pain so much. I remember her bowl of hot rice soup at midnight.
