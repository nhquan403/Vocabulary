Saving Money

When I got my first job after university, I was very excited about my salary. For the first time in my life, I had my own money. I did not have to ask my parents for anything. I could buy whatever I wanted. And that is exactly what I did. I bought new clothes, new shoes, and a new phone. I ate at nice restaurants with my friends almost every night. I drank expensive coffee every morning. I took taxis everywhere because I did not want to wait for the bus. At the end of every month, I looked at my bank account and saw that it was almost empty. Sometimes, I even had to borrow money from my friends to pay my rent.

For about a year, I did not think this was a problem. I was young, I had a job, and I was enjoying my life. Then, one day, something happened that changed my mind. My mother called me and told me that my father was sick and needed an operation. The operation was expensive, and my parents did not have enough money. They asked if I could help. I looked at my bank account. There was almost nothing in it. I felt terrible. I had earned a good salary for a whole year, and I had spent all of it on things I did not really need. In the end, my uncle lent my parents the money. My father had the operation, and he is fine now. But I never forgot how I felt that day. I promised myself that I would learn to save money.

The first thing I did was find out where my money was going. For one month, I wrote down everything I spent, every single coin. I used a simple app on my phone. Every time I bought something, I opened the app and typed the price and what I bought. At the end of the month, I looked at the results, and I was shocked. I had spent almost a third of my salary on eating out and coffee. I had spent a lot on taxis. And I had spent a lot on small things that I did not even remember buying: snacks, magazines, cheap clothes, phone games. Each thing was not very expensive, but together, they added up to a lot of money.

Next, I made a budget. A budget is a plan for how you will use your money each month. My cousin, who works in a bank, taught me a simple rule. You divide your income into three parts. About half of your money is for needs: rent, food, electricity, water, transport, and other things you must pay for. About thirty percent is for wants: eating out, entertainment, clothes, hobbies, and other things that make life fun but that you could live without. And at least twenty percent is for savings. "The most important thing," my cousin said, "is to pay yourself first. On the day you get your salary, put your savings in a separate account immediately. Then you live on the rest. If you wait until the end of the month to save, there will be nothing left."

I followed his advice. I opened a savings account at my bank, and every month, on the day I got my salary, I moved twenty percent of it into that account. At first, it was very hard. I felt like I had much less money to spend. But after a few months, I got used to it. And every month, I watched my savings grow. It made me feel proud and safe.

Then I started to look for ways to spend less. The biggest change was food. Instead of eating out every day, I started to cook at home. At first, I was not a very good cook. I burned the rice, I put too much salt in the soup, and once I almost set my kitchen on fire. But I watched cooking videos online, I called my mother for recipes, and slowly, I got better. Now I cook dinner almost every night, and I take my lunch to work in a lunchbox. It is cheaper, healthier, and often more delicious than restaurant food. On Sundays, I cook a big pot of soup or stew that lasts for three or four days. My colleagues were surprised when I started bringing my lunch. Now, some of them do it too.

I also stopped buying expensive coffee every morning. Instead, I bought a small coffee maker and made my own coffee at home. It costs about one tenth of the price of a coffee from a cafe. I still go to cafes with my friends sometimes, on the weekend, but now it is a special treat, not an everyday habit. And I enjoy it more because of that.

Transport was another big expense. I stopped taking taxis and started taking the bus. I bought a monthly bus pass, which is very cheap. The bus is slower than a taxi, but I use the time to read or listen to music. For short distances, I walk or ride a bicycle. This also helps me stay healthy. I have lost three kilos since I started walking more.

I also changed the way I shop. Before, I went shopping when I was bored or sad, and I often bought things just because they were on sale. Now, I have some rules. First, I make a shopping list before I go, and I only buy what is on the list. Second, if I want something expensive, I wait for one week before I buy it. If I still want it after a week, and I have enough money in my budget, I buy it. But most of the time, after a week, I realize that I do not really need it. Third, I compare prices at different shops and on the internet before I buy anything big. And fourth, I take care of the things I already have. I fix my clothes when they get a small hole, instead of throwing them away. I keep my phone in a strong case so it does not break.

Some of my friends thought I was becoming boring. "You never come out with us anymore," they said. It is true that I said no to many dinners and parties at first. But then I found other ways to have fun with my friends that did not cost much money. We started having picnics in the park, cooking dinner together at someone's house, playing board games, or going for long walks around the city. These activities were cheap or free, and they were often more fun than going to expensive restaurants. We talked more, laughed more, and became closer friends.

It was not always easy. There were times when I really wanted to buy something that I could not afford. There were times when my friends went on an expensive trip and I had to stay at home. There were times when I felt that I was missing out on life. But every time I felt like this, I remembered the phone call from my mother, and I remembered how terrible I felt when I could not help my family. That memory gave me the strength to continue.

After one year, I had saved enough money to cover three months of my living costs. This is called an emergency fund. My cousin told me that everyone should have one. "Life is full of surprises," he said. "You might lose your job, get sick, or need to help your family. An emergency fund means you will not have to borrow money or panic when something bad happens." Having this money in the bank made me feel much calmer and more confident about the future.

After two years, I had saved even more. I used some of it to take my parents on a holiday to the beach. It was the first holiday they had taken in more than ten years. My father cried a little when I told him. "We should be taking care of you," he said, "not the other way around." I told him that it was my turn now. It was the best money I have ever spent.

Today, I am still saving. I am not rich, and I still live a simple life. But I do not worry about money as much as I used to. I have learned that saving money is not about never spending anything. It is about spending on the things that really matter to you, and not wasting money on things that don't. It is about thinking before you buy. It is about being ready for the future. And most of all, it is about freedom: the freedom to help the people you love, to follow your dreams, and to sleep well at night without worrying about money.

If you want to start saving, start small. Write down everything you spend for one month. Make a simple budget. Save a little every month, even if it is only a small amount. Cook at home more often. Think before you buy. You will be surprised how quickly the small changes add up.
