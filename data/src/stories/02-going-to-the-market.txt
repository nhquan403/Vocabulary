Going to the Market

Every Saturday morning, I go to the local market with my grandmother. It is a small tradition between the two of us, and I enjoy it very much. During the week, I am busy with school and work, so I do not have much time to spend with her. But Saturday morning belongs to us. We wake up early, put on comfortable clothes, take two big shopping bags, and walk to the market together.

The market is about ten minutes from our house. We walk along a narrow street with small shops on both sides. There is a bakery, a hair salon, a shop that sells phones, and a small cafe where old men sit and drink coffee every morning. My grandmother says hello to everyone we pass. She has lived in this neighborhood for more than forty years, so she knows almost everybody.

When we arrive at the market, it is already busy and noisy. People are walking in every direction. Sellers are calling out to customers. Motorbikes are trying to get through the crowd. The air is full of different smells: fresh fish, ripe fruit, cooked meat, flowers, and spices. For some people, the market is too crowded and too loud. But for me, it is one of the most interesting places in the city. It is full of life.

My grandmother always has a plan. Before we leave home, she writes a shopping list on a small piece of paper. She keeps the list in her pocket, and she checks it many times while we shop. Today, the list says: rice, eggs, pork, fish, tomatoes, green vegetables, onions, garlic, bananas, and a chicken for Sunday dinner.

We start with the vegetables. There are many vegetable sellers at the market, but my grandmother always goes to the same woman. Her name is Mrs. Lan, and she has sold vegetables at this market for twenty years. Her vegetables come from her own small farm outside the city. They are not the cheapest, but they are always fresh and clean.

"Good morning, Grandma Hoa!" Mrs. Lan says with a big smile. "And good morning to you too, Mai. You are getting taller every week!"

My grandmother looks at the vegetables carefully. She picks up a bunch of green leaves, smells them, and checks the color. Then she looks at the tomatoes. She chooses the red, round ones and puts them in a small plastic bag. "How much are the tomatoes today?" she asks.

"Twenty thousand for a kilo," Mrs. Lan answers.

"Twenty thousand? Last week, they were only fifteen thousand!" my grandmother says.

Mrs. Lan laughs. "The weather has been very hot this month, and there are not many tomatoes. Everything is more expensive now. But for you, I can make it eighteen thousand."

My grandmother smiles and nods. This is a game they play every week. My grandmother always asks for a lower price, and Mrs. Lan always gives her a small discount. In the end, they are both happy. Mrs. Lan also puts a few extra green onions and some herbs into our bag for free. "For your soup," she says.

Next, we go to the meat section. This part of the market is not my favorite. It is hot and wet, and there are many flies. But my grandmother does not care. She walks straight to a tall man with a big knife. He is the pork seller, and his name is Mr. Binh. He cuts the meat very quickly and very well.

"I need half a kilo of pork, please," my grandmother says. "Not too fat. It is for my grandson. He doesn't like fat."

Mr. Binh chooses a nice piece of meat, cuts it, and puts it on the scale. "That is exactly half a kilo," he says. My grandmother watches the scale carefully. She trusts Mr. Binh, but she always checks. She says that you must always pay attention when you spend money.

After the pork, we buy a chicken. My grandmother wants a chicken from the countryside, not a chicken from a big farm. She says that countryside chickens have more flavor, because they walk around freely and eat natural food. They are more expensive, but she thinks they are worth the money. We will cook the chicken with ginger and rice tomorrow, when my uncle and his family come to visit.

Then we go to the fish section. The fish sellers keep the fish in big plastic boxes full of water. Some of the fish are still alive and moving. My grandmother points at one of them. "That one, please," she says. The fish seller catches it with a net, cleans it, and cuts it into pieces. My grandmother will use it to make sour fish soup, which is my father's favorite dish.

By now, our bags are getting heavy. I carry the heavier bag, and my grandmother carries the lighter one. She says she can carry both, but I don't let her. She is seventy-five years old, and I want to take care of her, just like she has always taken care of me.

We still need eggs, rice, and fruit. The egg seller is a young woman with a baby on her back. She sells chicken eggs and duck eggs. My grandmother buys ten chicken eggs. The young woman puts them in a paper bag very carefully, so they don't break. The baby looks at me with big black eyes, and I wave at her. She smiles and hides her face behind her mother's shoulder.

For the rice, we go to an old man who has a small shop at the edge of the market. He sells many different kinds of rice in big bags: white rice, brown rice, sticky rice, and black rice. My grandmother always buys five kilos of white rice. The old man weighs the rice and puts it in a strong bag. Rice is very heavy, so he offers to bring it to our house later on his bike. My grandmother thanks him and pays him.

Finally, we go to the fruit section. This is my favorite part of the market. There are so many colors! There are yellow bananas, orange mangoes, green watermelons, purple grapes, red apples, and many fruits that I don't know the names of in English. My grandmother buys a bunch of bananas and a big papaya. I ask her if we can also buy some mangoes, and she says yes. She knows that I love mangoes more than any other fruit.

While we are walking to the exit, we see a woman selling hot sticky rice with beans. My grandmother looks at me and smiles. "Are you hungry?" she asks. I say yes, of course. She buys two small packs of sticky rice, wrapped in banana leaves. We sit on two little plastic chairs next to the seller and eat our breakfast. The sticky rice is warm, soft, and a little sweet. It is simple food, but it tastes wonderful.

While we eat, my grandmother tells me about the market in the past. "When I was a young woman," she says, "this market was much smaller. There was no roof. When it rained, we got wet. There were no plastic bags. People brought their own baskets. And everything was very cheap. One egg cost less than a cup of tea!"

I like to hear her stories. They help me understand how much our city has changed. Today, there are big supermarkets everywhere. Many young people prefer to shop at supermarkets, because they are clean, cool, and comfortable. You can find everything in one place, and you don't need to ask about prices. Some people even buy their food online and never go to the market at all.

I asked my grandmother once why she still goes to the market instead of the supermarket. She thought for a moment and then said, "At the supermarket, you buy food. At the market, you meet people. Mrs. Lan, Mr. Binh, the egg seller, the old rice man, they are not just sellers. They are my friends. I know their children's names. When I was sick last year, Mrs. Lan came to my house with a bag of vegetables and a pot of soup. A supermarket cannot do that."

I think about her words as we walk home with our heavy bags. The sun is higher now, and the street is getting hot. My arms are tired, but I feel happy. I know that one day my grandmother will be too old to come to the market. When that day comes, I will continue our Saturday tradition. I will walk to the market, say hello to Mrs. Lan, buy the freshest vegetables, and ask for a small discount, just like my grandmother taught me.
